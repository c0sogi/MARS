Based on the task description and dataset characteristics, I propose the following baseline approach. This solution prioritizes computational efficiency and simplicity while effectively addressing the sequential and time-sensitive nature of fashion recommendations.

# Baseline Approach: Time-Aware Sequential Transition Graph

### Model
The architecture is a **probabilistic directed graph** (a first-order Markov Chain) where nodes represent distinct `article_id`s and edges represent the conditional probability of purchasing a specific item immediately after another. Unlike complex neural networks, this model explicitly learns sequential purchase patterns (e.g., "Users who buy Item A often buy Item B next"). To account for the fast-changing nature of fashion trends, the edge weights are **time-decayed**, ensuring that recent transitions influence the model significantly more than older ones. This synthesizes the sequential nature of **Model 2 (Transformer)** with the lightweight, co-occurrence logic of **Model 5**, formulated as a sparse graph structure similar to **Model 3 (LightGCN)** but without the heavy message-passing overhead.

### Data
1.  **Filtering**: Load the `transactions_train.csv` and filter for the last 8-12 weeks of data. Fashion is highly seasonal, and older data may introduce noise.
2.  **Sequence Generation**: Group transactions by `customer_id` and sort them by `t_dat`. Create ordered lists of purchased `article_id`s for each user.
3.  **Pair Construction**: Generate source-target pairs $(item_t, item_{t+1})$ from these sequences.
4.  **Time Weighting**: Assign a weight $w$ to each pair based on the date of the transaction $t$. A simple linear or exponential decay function (e.g., $w = \frac{1}{1 + \alpha \cdot \text{days\_ago}}$) should be used so that pairs from the most recent week have the highest influence.

### Training
The training procedure is a deterministic, single-pass counting operation, avoiding expensive gradient descent:
1.  **Aggregation**: Aggregate the weighted counts of all unique $(item_{source}, item_{target})$ pairs across all users.
2.  **Matrix Construction**: Construct a sparse adjacency matrix $M$ of size $N \times N$ (where $N$ is the number of unique articles), where $M_{ij}$ contains the aggregated weight of transitioning from item $i$ to item $j$.
3.  **Normalization**: Row-normalize the matrix such that each row sums to 1 (or represents relative likelihoods). This yields the transition probabilities $P(item_{next} | item_{current})$.
4.  **Global Popularity Calculation**: Separately, compute a "General Popularity" list by summing the time-decayed sales counts of all items. This serves as a fallback for cold-start users.

### Evaluation
1.  **History Retrieval**: For each `customer_id` in `sample_submission.csv`, retrieve their most recent purchase (or last $k$ purchases) from the training data.
2.  **Graph Query**: Look up the last purchased item(s) in the Transition Graph to retrieve the top connected neighbors (items with the highest transition probability). Rank these candidates by their probability scores.
3.  **Fallback & Padding**:
    *   If the user has no purchase history (Cold Start), predict the top-12 items from the **Global Popularity** list.
    *   If the Graph Query returns fewer than 12 candidates, pad the remaining slots with items from the **Global Popularity** list, filtering out duplicates.
4.  **Submission**: Format the top-12 ranked `article_id`s as a space-separated string for each customer and save to CSV.
